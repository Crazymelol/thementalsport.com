How to handle tryout nerves is simple: focus on what you can control, reset your breath right before you step onto the field, and play aggressive instead of safe. Drop the doubt, lock in the routine, and let the selectors see effort and attitude as much as skill.

## Why tryout nerves wreck performance  
Nerves tighten the shoulders, shrink the stride, and mute the instinct that made you great in practice. When anxiety spikes, the brain defaults to the “protect‑me” mode. You start second‑guessing every pass, every move, and the result is tentative play that looks cheap to a coach. The key is to recognize the pattern early and intervene before the nervous loop locks you down.

## What selectors actually watch  
Coaches have a short window to decide who belongs on the squad. They scan for:

1. **Effort level** – players who hustle, chase loose balls, and keep the intensity high.  
2. **Attitude** – willingness to take on challenges, respond to feedback, and stay positive after a mistake.  
3. **Decision speed** – athletes who act quickly, even if the choice isn’t perfect, show confidence.  
4. **Team fit** – players who communicate, support teammates, and raise the collective energy.

Skill matters, but a player who appears nervous and plays safe looks less reliable than a slightly less polished athlete who attacks the game with purpose.

## Control the controllables: mindset, breathing, aggression  

### Adopt a growth mindset  
Carol Dweck’s research shows that believing abilities can improve fuels persistence. Tell yourself that the tryout is a learning opportunity, not a life‑or‑death test. This mental shift reduces the fear of failure and opens space for aggressive play.

### Use the physiological sigh to reset  
Andrew Huberman explains that a double inhale followed by a long exhale drops CO₂ levels and calms the nervous system within seconds. Before you walk onto the field, take two quick breaths through the nose, then exhale slowly through the mouth. Do it three times and feel the tension melt.

### Play‑aggressive rule  
When you feel the urge to “play it safe,” flip a mental switch: **attack every ball, challenge every opponent, and commit fully to each action**. The rule is binary – either you are aggressive or you are not. It removes the gray area where hesitation lives. Aggression here means purposeful, not reckless. It signals to the coach that you trust your training and are ready to contribute.

## Build a pre‑game routine that locks in performance  
A consistent routine tells your brain that it’s time to perform. The article on [how to build a pre‑game routine](/blog/how-to-build-a-pre-game-routine) breaks down the steps for creating a ritual that cues focus, confidence, and calm. Stick to the same sequence each day: light jog, dynamic stretches, breath reset, and a quick visualisation of your best play. The repetition makes the nervous system treat the tryout like any other practice session.

## The tryout‑day plan (numbered protocol)  

1. **Morning prep** – Eat a balanced meal, hydrate, and avoid caffeine spikes. A stable blood‑sugar level keeps anxiety in check.  
2. **Warm‑up** – Complete a dynamic warm‑up that mirrors the sport’s movements. This raises core temperature and primes motor pathways.  
3. **Breathing reset** – Perform the physiological sigh three times right after the warm‑up. Feel the chest expand, then release tension.  
4. **Visualise the aggressive play** – Spend 30 seconds picturing yourself charging the ball, making a decisive pass, or taking the shot. Keep the image vivid and fast‑paced.  
5. **Team‑huddle mindset check** – Remind yourself of the three selectors’ priorities: effort, attitude, and decision speed. Align your intention with those cues.  
6. **First drill – execute the play‑aggressive rule** – As soon as the drill starts, commit to the rule. If a pass feels risky, take it. If a defender is close, push forward. The moment you act, nervous chatter quiets.  
7. **Mid‑session quick reset** – After the first half of the tryout, step aside, sip water, and repeat the breath reset. This prevents the nervous system from building up fatigue.  
8. **Second drill – showcase effort** – Sprint extra meters, dive for loose balls, and shout supportive words to teammates. Effort is visible even from the sidelines.  
9. **Cool‑down mental debrief** – While you stretch, run through what went well and what you’ll tweak next time. Keep the tone constructive; avoid self‑criticism.  
10. **Post‑tryout recovery** – Eat protein, hydrate, and log a brief note of the day’s highlights. This closes the loop and prepares you for the next challenge.

Follow this plan each tryout and you’ll notice a steadier heart rate, clearer decisions, and a louder presence on the field.

## Stop choking under pressure  
If you find yourself freezing mid‑play, the guide on [how to stop choking under pressure](/blog/how-to-stop-choking-under-pressure) offers practical tricks: break the action into micro‑steps, use self‑distanced self‑talk (Ethan Kross), and anchor your focus on the next immediate task rather than the outcome. Pair those tactics with the aggressive rule and you eliminate the “what‑if” loop that triggers choking.

## Resources you can trust  
Giannis Notaras’ book *Overcoming Mental Blocks* walks athletes through common mental barriers and provides drills to dismantle them. It’s a solid reference if you want deeper exercises beyond the day‑of plan.

---

**Takeaway:** Nerves are a signal, not a verdict. Reset your breath, lock into the play‑aggressive rule, and let effort speak louder than doubt. The selectors will notice the attitude you bring, and the skill will follow.
