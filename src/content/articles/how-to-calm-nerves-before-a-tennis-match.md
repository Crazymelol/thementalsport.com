How to calm nerves before a tennis match starts with a simple, repeatable process: a four‑stage between‑point routine, a focused breathing reset on changeovers, a concise serve ritual, and a strict “play the next point only” mindset. Follow these steps and the anxiety that tightens your serve will dissolve.

## Why the pause between points feels like a pressure cooker  

Every rally ends with a brief silence. In that silence your brain flips from action to analysis, and worry sneaks in. Studies show that the brain’s default mode activates during idle moments, pulling up past mistakes and future fears. The result is a spike in heart rate and a tightening grip on the racquet. Recognizing the pause as a tool, not a threat, flips the script.

## The four‑stage between‑point routine  

A routine gives the brain a script to follow, replacing rumination with action. The stages are quick, observable, and can be practiced on court or in practice.

1. **Reset the body** – Drop your shoulders, roll your shoulders back, and take a short, sharp exhale. This signals the nervous system to shift out of fight‑or‑flight.  
2. **Visual cue** – Pick a small, consistent visual cue: the seam of the ball, the line on the court, or the color of your shoes. Stare at it for one second.  
3. **Positive cue word** – Say a single word that means “focus” to you, “steady,” “ready,” or “now.” Keep it low‑volume; the goal is internal, not theatrical.  
4. **Plan the next point** – State the first tactical move you will make: “attack the backhand,” “serve wide,” or “play the crosscourt forehand.”  

Repeat the four steps in the same order for every point. Consistency trains muscle memory and tells the brain exactly what to expect.

## Breathing reset on the changeover  

A changeover gives you 90 seconds, enough time for a physiological sigh that resets the autonomic nervous system. Andrew Huberman describes the sigh as a double inhale followed by a slow exhale. Here’s how to embed it:

- Inhale through the nose for a count of 2.  
- Add a second, quick inhale to fill the lungs fully.  
- Exhale through the mouth for a count of 4‑5, feeling the ribs collapse.  

Do this twice during the changeover. The first sigh clears lingering tension; the second steadies your heart rate. If you need a reminder, check out the **[box breathing technique for athletes](/blog/box-breathing-for-athletes)** for a structured alternative.

## Serve ritual: the 5‑second launch pad  

Serving is the only time you control the entire point from the ground up. A short ritual keeps nerves in check and locks in mechanics.

1. **Foot placement** – Position your feet exactly as you did in practice.  
2. **Grip check** – Feel the handle, adjust if needed, then close your hand.  
3. **Toss anchor** – Visualize the toss height you want, then lift the ball with a smooth, upward motion.  
4. **Breath cue** – Take a single, quiet inhale before the swing.  
5. **Swing trigger** – On the exhale, execute the swing, focusing only on the contact point.  

Practice this sequence repeatedly in drills until it becomes automatic. When nerves rise, the ritual pulls you back into the familiar motor pattern.

## Playing the next point only  

The mental habit of “one point at a time” prevents the mind from spiraling into past errors or future worries. Implement it with a simple mental switch:

- After the point ends, say “next” in your head.  
- If a negative thought tries to linger, acknowledge it with a brief “noticed” and let it drift.  
- Return instantly to the four‑stage routine.  

Ethan Kross’s research on self‑distanced self‑talk supports this: viewing thoughts as an observer reduces emotional impact. The “next” cue works as that observer.

## Building a pre‑match foundation  

A solid pre‑game routine primes the nervous system before you step onto the court. The routine should include:

- Light dynamic warm‑up to raise body temperature.  
- A brief mental rehearsal of the four‑stage routine and serve ritual.  
- A final check of equipment and hydration.  

For a detailed template, see the **[pre‑game routine guide](/blog/how-to-build-a-pre-game-routine)**.

## Putting it all together: a day‑to‑day checklist  

| Moment | Action |
|--------|--------|
| **Morning** | Review visual cue and cue word; run through the four‑stage routine mentally. |
| **Pre‑practice** | Run the serve ritual 5 times, focusing on breath cue. |
| **During practice** | Apply the between‑point routine on every rally; use the physiological sigh on changeovers. |
| **Night before match** | Read a chapter from *mental‑blocks* by Giannis Notaras to reinforce mindset. |
| **Match day** | Follow the pre‑game routine, then lock into the four‑stage and serve ritual from the first point. |

Consistency across days builds a neural pathway that overrides the stress response. When the pathway is strong, nerves lose their grip.

## Common pitfalls and how to avoid them  

- **Skipping the visual cue** – The brain craves a concrete anchor. If you forget, the routine stalls and anxiety resurfaces.  
- **Over‑thinking the breath** – The sigh is a tool, not a performance test. Keep it quick; any hesitation re‑activates tension.  
- **Changing the cue word** – Each word carries its own emotional baggage. Stick with the one that feels neutral and empowering.  
- **Rushing the serve ritual** – Speed invites error. Slow down the first three steps; speed will follow naturally on the swing.  

Address each pitfall as soon as you notice it. A quick self‑audit after a set can catch drift before it becomes habit.

## Quick reference: the 2‑minute match‑day cheat sheet  

1. **Arrive early**, hydrate, and do a 5‑minute dynamic warm‑up.  
2. **Run the four‑stage routine** twice, eyes closed, to set the mental tone.  
3. **Practice the serve ritual** three times, focusing on breath cue.  
4. **Step onto the court**, repeat the visual cue, and start the first point.  

If nerves spike, pause, perform a physiological sigh, and return to step 1 of the between‑point routine. Simplicity wins.

Bottom line: nerves are a signal, not a verdict. By giving them a script, four‑stage routine, breath reset, serve ritual, and a “next point only” mindset, you turn anxiety into a controllable beat. Train the script, trust the process, and watch your serve stay loose even when the score is tight.
