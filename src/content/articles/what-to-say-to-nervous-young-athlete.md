When your child is nervous before a game, don't say "relax" or "just have fun." Say something that validates the feeling and then points them at one thing they can control: "It makes sense you're nervous, this matters to you. Let's just take the first play." Nerves are not the enemy. Fighting them is.

"Relax" sounds kind. To a keyed-up kid it lands as "the way you feel right now is wrong," which adds a fresh worry on top of the one they already had. "Just have fun" does the same thing when they are too tight to feel fun anywhere nearby.

## Why Telling a Kid to Calm Down Backfires

You cannot talk a nervous system out of being activated. When you tell a nervous kid to relax, you are asking them to control something that is not under conscious control, and failing at it makes them more anxious, not less.

There is a better target. The body's state during nerves, fast heart rate, tight chest, buzzing hands, is almost identical to the state during excitement. Research on anxiety reappraisal shows that people told to say "I am excited" before a high-pressure task outperform people told to calm down. You are not removing the energy. You are relabeling it.

So the job is not to shrink the feeling. It is to give it a better name and a job to do.

## What to Say Instead

Lead with validation, then redirect to something small and controllable.

- Instead of "relax": "Your body's just getting ready. That buzzing means you care."
- Instead of "don't be nervous": "Nervous is normal. Every player out there feels some version of it."
- Instead of "you'll be fine": "I don't know how the game will go, and I love watching you play either way."
- Instead of "calm down": "Let's just win the first thirty seconds. Then the next."

That last move matters. A nervous mind is time-traveling to an imagined disaster. Pulling it back to the very next action, the first pass, the first serve, the opening whistle, shrinks the problem to a size a kid can handle.

## A Simple 3-Step Reset You Can Teach

Give your child a routine they own, so they are not dependent on you being there to say the right thing.

1. **Name it.** Out loud or in their head: "I'm nervous, and that's okay." Naming an emotion takes some of its heat out.
2. **Breathe it down.** One physiological sigh: two inhales through the nose (a normal breath, then a second short sip on top), followed by a long, slow exhale through the mouth. Two or three of these drop the heart rate faster than a single deep breath. It is the quickest reset an athlete has, and it is the same tool the pros use before a free throw or a start.
3. **Pick one job.** Choose a single controllable focus for the first minute: "get to every ball," "talk to my teammates," "move my feet." One job, not the whole game.

Practice this at home when the stakes are zero, so it is automatic when the stakes are high.

## Keep Your Own Nerves Off Their Plate

Kids read parents. If your jaw is tight and your questions are sharp on the drive in, your child inherits it. Your calm is contagious, and so is your anxiety. The steadiest thing you can offer a nervous athlete is a parent who clearly is not panicking about the outcome.

That includes the ride itself. A quiet, easy car with the radio on does more than a pep talk. For the wider before-and-after picture, [the car ride home rule](/blog/car-ride-home-rule) covers the trip both ways.

## Build the Kind of Confidence That Lowers Nerves

Nerves shrink when a kid has evidence they can handle hard moments. That evidence is built over time, through small wins and recoveries, not through one good speech. ## The Takeaway

Nervous is not broken. It is the feeling of a kid who cares about something, and your job is not to erase it but to help them carry it. Validate the feeling, hand them one small thing to control, teach them a breath they can take without you, and keep your own worry off their shoulders.
