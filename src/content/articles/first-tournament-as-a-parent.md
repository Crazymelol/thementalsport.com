If you are a first tournament parent, your job is to keep the day running smooth, keep your child fed, rested, and emotionally steady, and stay out of the coach’s way. The right routine between rounds, the right words after a game, and the right attitude on the sidelines turn a chaotic day into a learning experience.

## Food, Hydration, and Rest on a Hot Day  

Kids burn calories faster than adults when they sprint, jump, and focus under bright lights. Pack a cooler with water, a sports drink, and easy‑to‑eat carbs like bananas, granola bars, or a peanut‑butter sandwich. Offer a sip every 15‑20 minutes; dehydration spikes fatigue and irritability.  

Rest is not a luxury, it is a performance tool. Find a shaded bench or a quiet corner between matches. A 5‑minute seated break with eyes closed lets the nervous system reset. If the schedule allows, a short nap in a stroller or a reclined seat can shave minutes off recovery time.  

## The Between‑Rounds Routine (Step‑by‑Step)

A repeatable routine removes guesswork and eases anxiety. Call it the “Between‑Rounds Routine” and stick to it each time the whistle blows.

1. **Hydrate** – Give a quick drink, no more than a gulp, to avoid stomach upset.  
2. **Fuel** – Hand over a bite sized snack; aim for a mix of carbs and protein.  
3. **Breath Reset** – Lead a physiological sigh: inhale deeply through the nose, fill the lungs, then exhale slowly through the mouth twice. Andrew Huberman describes this as a fast way to calm the nervous system.  
4. **Mind Check** – Prompt a self‑distanced self‑talk moment. Ask your child, “What’s one thing you did well in the last game?” Ethan Kross shows that stepping back from the moment reduces rumination.  
5. **Gear Check** – Verify shoes are tied, socks are dry, and any equipment is in place. A quick visual scan prevents a scramble later.  
6. **Positive Cue** – Use a short phrase like “Ready, steady, go.” Keep it consistent; the brain learns to associate the cue with focus.  

Repeat the cycle after every match. Consistency builds a mental anchor that steadies performance despite heat or crowd noise.

## What to Say Between Games  

Words matter, but they must be measured. After a win, a quick “Great effort, you kept your head up” reinforces a growth mindset (Carol Dweck). After a loss, avoid “You should have…”. Instead, ask a reflective question: “What did you notice about your positioning?” This invites problem solving without blame.  

If your child looks frustrated, try a self‑distanced phrase: “It’s a game, not a verdict.” It helps them see the event as a temporary state, not a permanent judgment. For more ideas, see the article on [what to say after a game](/blog/what-to-say-after-a-game).  

## Staying Off the Coaching Sidelines  

Your instinct is to correct technique or call out strategy, but the coach already has a plan. Interfering can confuse the athlete and undermine authority. Trust the coach’s instructions, and trust your child to execute them.  

If you notice a safety issue, like a loose shoe lace, step in calmly. Otherwise, keep your voice low and your focus on logistics. The Sports Parents Playbook outlines dozens of scenarios where restraint wins the day; you can read more in the [sports parents playbook](/blog/sports-parents-playbook).  

## Quick Checklist for the First Tournament Day  

- Cooler with water, sports drink, and snacks  
- Shade or a quiet spot for rest  
- Pre‑packed extra socks and shoe ties  
- List of short, positive cue phrases  
- Phone set to silent, only for emergencies  
- A copy of the tournament schedule (paper works best in heat)  

Carry this list in a small bag. Tick each item as you set up the day. The act of checking off reinforces confidence and reduces panic.

## Training the Mind for Competition  

Beyond the day itself, building mental stamina helps your child handle pressure. Giannis Notaras’s *Nurturing Self‑Worth* offers exercises that develop inner confidence without relying on external validation. Incorporate a 5‑minute visualization before bedtime: picture the tournament layout, the sound of the whistle, and the feeling of a steady breath. Over weeks, the brain learns to treat the competition environment as familiar ground.

## Takeaway  

Keep food simple, keep rest intentional, keep words brief, and keep coaching to the sidelines. A repeatable between‑rounds routine turns chaos into rhythm, and your calm presence becomes the quiet engine that powers your child’s performance.
