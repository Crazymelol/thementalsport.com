The car ride home is the most damaging twenty minutes in youth sports, and it has nothing to do with the score. Surveys of youth and college athletes keep pointing to the same answer: the worst part of playing wasn't losing, it was the ride home with mom or dad. Say less, and let your athlete decide if the game gets discussed at all.

## Why Is the Car Ride Home So Hard on Young Athletes?

Right after a game, your kid is still running hot. Adrenaline is up, emotions are close to the surface, and whatever happened out there, good or bad, hasn't settled yet.

Then they get in a car with you. No bench to sit on, no teammates to disappear into, no coach to absorb the conversation. Just you, them, and a windshield, for ten or twenty straight minutes.

You've been watching too. Maybe you saw the missed assignment, the bad line change, the shot they should have taken. You want to help, so you start talking before the seatbelt clicks.

That's the mistake. Not because you're wrong about the game. Because the timing turns a parent into a second coach at the exact moment your kid needed a parent.

## What's the Difference Between a Coach's Job and a Parent's Job?

A coach's job is to evaluate performance: who played well, what needs fixing, who starts next week. That's supposed to be a little transactional. It's the job.

A parent's job is different. It's to show up for a kid whether they scored twice or fouled out, whether the team won or got run off the field. That support isn't supposed to have conditions attached to it.

The car ride home goes wrong when those two jobs get mixed up. A parent who critiques the game in the car has, in that moment, stopped being a parent and started being an unpaid second coach, one the kid never signed up for and can't get away from because the doors are locked.

If you also coach the team, this matters even more. Your kid needs at least one adult whose approval doesn't move with the scoreboard. If you're both coach and parent, the car has to be where the parent shows up.

## What to Say on the Ride Home After a Game

Bruce Brown and Rob Miller have spent years surveying college athletes about what their parents got right and wrong during their youth sports days. One answer keeps showing up, across athlete after athlete, as the single sentence they most wanted to hear: "I love to watch you play."

Not "great game." Not "you should have passed it." Just that. Six words that tell a kid their value in your eyes has nothing to do with the stat sheet.

Say it, then stop talking. Resist the follow-up. It works because it isn't an opening line for a conversation about performance. It's the whole message.

## The 24-Minute Rule for the Drive Home

Call it the 24-Minute Rule, because for a lot of families that's roughly how long the drive from the field to the driveway takes. For that window, your job isn't to coach. It's to be a parent.

1. **Greet them with your body, not a question.** A hug, a high five, a hand on the shoulder. Save "how do you feel about that?" for later, if ever.
2. **Let the first few minutes be quiet.** Silence in the car isn't awkward. It's your kid's nervous system coming down off the game. Don't fill it.
3. **Offer food or a drink before you offer feedback.** A hungry, dehydrated kid processes a hard loss worse than a fed one. Handle the body before the debrief.
4. **Say the line, then stop.** "I love to watch you play." Nothing added, nothing walked back.
5. **Let them decide if the game gets discussed.** If they bring it up, follow. If they don't, don't go looking for it.

### Say This, Not That

1. Say "I love to watch you play." Not "you should have shot it more."
2. Say "Want to grab something to eat?" Not "let's talk about those turnovers."
3. Say nothing for the first five minutes. Not "so what happened out there?"
4. Say "That sounds frustrating." Not "here's what you did wrong."
5. Say "Do you want to talk about it?" Not launching into your own analysis unasked.

## A Short Script for the Drive Home

Parent: (hug) "Hey. Proud of you. Radio on or off?"

Athlete: "Off is fine."

(A few quiet minutes pass.)

Athlete: "Coach barely played me in the second half."

Parent: "That sounds frustrating. You want to talk about it, or let it sit for now?"

Athlete: "Let it sit, I guess."

Parent: "Okay. I love to watch you play. You hungry?"

Notice what didn't happen. The parent didn't defend the coach, didn't pile on, didn't turn one sentence from the kid into a full debrief. They reflected it back, offered an out, and moved toward food. That's the whole job.

## What If They Want to Talk About It?

Everything above assumes quiet is the default. It isn't always. Some kids want to process out loud right away, and shutting that down is its own mistake.

The rule isn't "never talk in the car." It's "let them lead." If your athlete brings up the game, your job is to ask questions, not deliver verdicts: "What was that like?" "What do you want to work on?" Not "here's what I saw."

The same restraint applies before a game, especially with a kid who gets anxious in the parking lot. If that's your athlete, [what to say to a nervous young athlete](/blog/what-to-say-to-nervous-young-athlete) walks through language that settles a kid down instead of adding pressure before the first whistle.

None of this is about pretending the game didn't happen. It's about choosing your moment. Twenty minutes after the final whistle, with adrenaline still running and a captive audience in the passenger seat, is close to the worst moment available. A day later, once they've slept on it and asked for your take, is a far better one.

## Say Less. Love More.

You don't have to get the car ride home perfect. You have to get it quiet.

Hug first. Talk later, only if they want to. Say the one line that matters, and let it be enough. The scoreboard will still be there tomorrow. Whether your kid still loves the game in five years depends more on what you don't say tonight than what you do.
