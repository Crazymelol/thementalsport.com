Positive self-talk for athletes works best when it is specific and instructional, not vague cheerleading. "You've got this" fades under pressure. "Smooth, eyes up, next play" gives your brain an actual instruction. Below are 12 scripts you can steal, grouped by the moment you need them, plus how to build your own cue word.

Your inner voice is talking whether you manage it or not. Left alone, under pressure it usually turns critical. The skill is not silencing it. It is giving it a script.

## Instructional vs Motivational: Use the Right Tool

Two kinds of self-talk do two different jobs. Studies on self-talk show instructional cues (aim, technique, focus) help most with skill and precision, while motivational cues (effort, intensity) help most with power and endurance.

- **Precision task** (free throw, putt, serve toss): go instructional. "Smooth. Follow through."
- **Effort task** (last sprint, final set, loose ball): go motivational. "Go. Now. Attack."

Matching the cue to the moment matters more than how loud or hyped it is.

## 12 Scripts That Work

### Before the game (settle and prime)
1. "This is where I want to be." Reframes pressure as a chance, not a threat.
2. "I've done the work. Trust it." Points you at preparation, not the unknown.
3. "Loose and low." A body cue that drops tension before it starts.

### After a mistake (reset fast)
4. "Next play." The single most useful phrase in sports. The last one is gone.
5. "Flush it." Pair it with one hard exhale and move on.
6. "So what, now what." Acknowledges the error without dwelling, then redirects.

### When you're behind or tired (find another gear)
7. "One point at a time." Shrinks an overwhelming deficit to something winnable.
8. "Win the next 30 seconds." A target small enough to actually chase.
9. "Empty the tank." Motivational fuel for the effort tasks at the end.

### When nerves hit (relabel the energy)
10. "I'm excited, not scared." The body feels the same; the label changes performance.
11. "Feet moving." Action beats rumination. Give the nerves a job.
12. "Just the first one." Focus on the opening play, not the whole game.

Write your top three on a wristband or your water bottle. Cues you have decided on in advance beat anything you try to invent mid-panic.

## Talk to Yourself by Name

One upgrade backed by research: use your own name or "you" instead of "I." Ethan Kross's work on self-distanced self-talk found that "You're fine, Sam. Reset and go" creates enough distance from the emotion to think clearly, while "I'm fine" keeps you stuck inside it. It sounds strange. It works. Coaching yourself in the second person is the same trick that steadies you [after a mistake spirals into a bad game](/blog/how-to-bounce-back-after-a-bad-game).

## Build Your Personal Cue Word

A cue word is a shortcut to your best state. Building one takes repetition.

1. **Name your best state.** Loose? Sharp? Locked in? Pick the feeling you want on demand.
2. **Choose one short word for it.** "Smooth," "loose," "now," "hunt." One syllable or two.
3. **Pair it in practice.** Every time you hit that state in training, say the word. You are wiring the word to the feeling.
4. **Trigger it in games.** Once linked, the word starts pulling the state back for you.

This is the same mechanism behind calming a young athlete with a rehearsed phrase, covered in [what to say to a nervous young athlete](/blog/what-to-say-to-nervous-young-athlete). Rehearsed language works because it is automatic when it counts.

## Kill the Critic Without Faking Positivity

Fake positivity ("everything's great!") does not hold up when you are down by ten. The goal is not delusion. It is redirection. When the critic starts ("you always choke here"), you do not argue with it. You replace it with an instruction: "Eyes up. Next play." You cannot think two things at once, so give the mind a job and the criticism loses the floor.

## The Takeaway

Skip "think positive." Build a small set of specific cue words, match instructional cues to skill moments and motivational cues to effort moments, coach yourself by name, and decide your phrases before you need them. Your inner voice is going to talk the whole game. Hand it a script.
