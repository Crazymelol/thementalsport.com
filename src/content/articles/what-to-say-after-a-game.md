The best thing to say to your child after a game is short, unconditional, and about them rather than the scoreboard: "I love to watch you play." Then stop, hand them a snack, and let them lead. The game just happened to them, so the first move is yours to make small.

Most parents mean well and still get this wrong. They open the car door with "what happened on that last play?" before their kid has caught their breath. The intention is help. The message a kid hears is that your interest rides on how they performed.

## Why the First Ten Minutes Matter So Much

Right after a game your child is flooded: adrenaline, disappointment or relief, the replay of their own mistakes already looping. Their thinking brain is offline. Nothing you teach in that window sticks, and anything critical you say lands three times harder than you meant it.

So the goal for those first ten minutes is not to coach. It is to be a safe place to land. The feedback can wait. The connection cannot.

## The Five Things Worth Saying

Keep a short list in your head so you are not improvising while your own emotions are up.

1. **"I love to watch you play."** The classic for a reason. It separates your love from their result.
2. **"That looked really fun / really hard out there."** Names what they felt without judging it.
3. **"What was the best part for you?"** Lets them relive something good and hands them the wheel.
4. **"I'm proud of how you kept going after that rough patch."** Praises effort and response, not the scoreline.
5. **"Want to talk about it, or just get food?"** Gives them a real choice and respects a no.

Notice none of these grade the performance. That is the point.

## The Phrases to Cut

- "You should have..." Anything starting this way is a critique wearing a helpful costume.
- "Why didn't you..." Same move, now as a question they cannot answer without feeling worse.
- "The ref cost you that." Teaches them to blame instead of adjust.
- "Did you see how well [teammate] played?" Comparison is a slow leak in a kid's confidence.

If a sentence would sting coming from a coach right after a loss, it stings worse from a parent.

## Praise Effort, Not the Scoreboard

Carol Dweck's work on growth mindset is the useful backbone here. Kids praised for effort and process keep taking risks. Kids praised for being "a natural" start protecting the label and avoid anything that might threaten it.

So aim your words at things your child controlled. "You chased down every loose ball" beats "you're so talented." One is repeatable on purpose. The other is a trait they will feel pressure to keep proving.

This is the same principle behind [the six words that build confident young athletes](/blog/six-words-confident-young-athletes): tie your approval to how they competed, not what the scoreboard said.

## Scripts for the Hard Ones

**After a tough individual game (they played badly):**
"Hey. That was a frustrating one, huh? ... I love watching you compete. You hungry?" Then quiet. Do not fill the silence with fixes.

**After a loss where they gave everything:**
"You left it all out there. That's the whole job. Proud of you." No "but next time."

**After a win where they were selfish or coasted:**
Still not the moment. Celebrate the win now. If there is a real conversation about attitude, it happens a day later, calm, and as a question: "How did you feel about how you played today?"

The pattern holds across all three: connect first, coach later, and only if invited. For why the drive itself is the danger zone, see [the car ride home rule](/blog/car-ride-home-rule).

## When Does the Real Conversation Happen?

Later. Hours later, or the next day. Once the emotion has drained, most kids will bring the game up on their own, and that is your opening. When they do, ask more than you tell: "What do you want to work on this week?" gets a better answer than "here's what I saw."

## The Takeaway

Your kid will forget most of the scores. They will not forget how it felt to get in the car with you after they lost. Make that feeling safe, keep the first ten minutes about them and not the game, and you buy yourself years of a kid who still wants you there.
