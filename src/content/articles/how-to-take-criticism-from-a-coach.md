How to take criticism from a coach is simple: listen, pause two seconds, then respond with a focused question. The goal isn’t to defend yourself, it’s to stay coachable and improve your game. Separate the feedback from your identity, and you’ll keep the coach’s advice flowing.

## Why you shut down when a coach corrects you  

Most athletes treat correction like a personal attack. The brain lights up the threat center, and the body goes into fight‑or‑flight. That reaction protects ego, not performance. When you feel judged, you instinctively block the message. Recognizing the physiological loop stops the spiral before it starts.

## Separate the correction from your self‑worth  

Your skill level is a variable, not a verdict. Think of feedback as data, not a judgment. Carol Dweck’s growth mindset research shows that viewing criticism as information fuels learning. Ask yourself: “Is this about my effort or about who I am?” If the answer is effort, you can act on it without bruising your confidence.

## The two‑second pause that keeps you coachable  

The moment a coach speaks, you have about two seconds before your defensive brain takes over. Use that gap to breathe, reset, and let the words settle. A brief pause creates mental distance, similar to Ethan Kross’s self‑distanced self‑talk technique. In practice, count “one‑two” silently, then let the coach finish.

## The Coachable Response: a step‑by‑step protocol  

**Step 1 – Acknowledge the input**  
Say “Got it” or nod. You’re not agreeing, you’re confirming you heard the message.  

**Step 2 – Pause two seconds**  
Take the breath, count “one‑two.” This stops the impulse to argue.  

**Step 3 – Restate the key point**  
Repeat the correction in your own words: “You want me to keep my elbow down on the pass, right?” This shows you’re processing.  

**Step 4 – Ask a clarifying question**  
Now you own the conversation. “What’s the best way to keep my elbow low when I’m under pressure?” The question signals openness and gives you actionable insight.  

**Step 5 – Commit to a micro‑adjustment**  
Pick one tiny change to try in the next drill. “I’ll focus on tucking my elbow for the next ten passes.” Small wins build confidence fast.

Follow this protocol each time you’re corrected. Consistency rewires the brain’s threat response into a learning loop.

## Ask the follow‑up question  

The follow‑up question is the secret weapon. It flips the dynamic from “coach vs. player” to “coach and player solving a problem together.” When you ask, you demonstrate respect for the coach’s expertise and invite a concrete tip. That exchange often leads to a specific drill, a visual cue, or a mental cue you can practice later.

## Practice the habit on the field  

You can’t master the coachable response in a meeting; you need to embed it in practice. Start each training session with a quick self‑check: “Am I ready to hear correction without defending?” When a coach points out a flaw, run through the five steps. Over weeks, the pause becomes instinctive, and the follow‑up question feels natural.

## When it still feels hard  

Even seasoned pros stumble. If you catch yourself reacting defensively, pause the drill, take a sip of water, and repeat the two‑second count. Remind yourself that the coach’s goal is your improvement, not a personal attack. Write down the criticism, your restated version, and the follow‑up question in a notebook. Seeing it on paper detaches the emotion.
