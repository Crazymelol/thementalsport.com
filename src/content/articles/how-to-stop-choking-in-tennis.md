How to stop choking in tennis starts with a simple rule: treat every point like any other, no extra story, no extra analysis. Strip the moment down to a repeatable between‑point routine, a serve ritual that survives pressure, and a laser focus on the target, not the mechanics. If you can lock those three habits, the choking cycle collapses.

## Why big points feel different  

Your brain treats a break point or a match point as a threat, not a challenge. The amygdala spikes, cortisol rises, and the part of you that normally runs the swing gets hijacked by doubt. The result is a tightened grip, a rushed motion, and a higher chance of a double fault. Recognizing that the feeling is physiological, not personal, is the first step out of the trap.

## The overthinking trap between points  

Tennis gives you up to 20 seconds between rallies. Most players fill that time with replay, self‑critique, or future planning. That mental chatter feeds the choke. Instead, use those seconds for a concrete, repeatable routine that tells your brain, “I’m done with the last point, I’m ready for the next.”  

### A three‑step between‑point routine  

1. **Reset breath** – Take a slow inhale through the nose for a count of four, then exhale through the mouth for a count of six. The physiological sigh, described by Andrew Huberman, drops heart rate instantly.  
2. **Anchor phrase** – Say a single word that means “reset” to you, such as “steady” or “ready.” Keep it quiet, keep it consistent.  
3. **Target visual** – Glance at the spot you want the ball to land on the opponent’s court, lock eyes on it for two seconds, then step into the next rally.  

Repeat this in the same order every time. Consistency trains the nervous system to switch off the threat response.

## Serve ritual that survives pressure  

Your serve is the only shot you start from scratch each point, so it needs a ritual that can’t be broken by nerves. Build a mini‑sequence that you perform the same way on every serve, even on a match point.

1. **Ball placement** – Toss the ball to the same height and angle every time.  
2. **Grip check** – Lightly feel the handle, confirm the continental grip, then let go.  
3. **Foot pivot** – Shift weight to the back foot, then step forward as you swing.  

When the routine is automatic, the brain has less room for doubt. If you need a deeper dive, the article on [how to stop choking under pressure](/blog/how-to-stop-choking-under-pressure) walks through the mental side of rituals.

## External focus: aim at the target, not the swing  

Self‑distanced self‑talk, a technique championed by Ethan Kross, works best when you direct attention outward. Instead of “don’t miss,” picture the corner of the service box you want to hit. Let the visual cue guide the swing. Studies show that external focus improves accuracy and reduces muscle tension.  

## Break the double‑fault spiral  

A double fault is often a mental loop: “I missed, I’ll try harder, I miss again.” Interrupt that loop with a “reset breath” before the second serve. Treat the second serve as a brand‑new point, not a correction of the first. Use the same anchor phrase you use between points; it tells the brain the error is irrelevant.

## Play the next point only  

When you finish a rally, your mind should automatically switch to “next point.” The phrase “one point at a time” works as a mental cue. If a mistake lingers, write it down on a notepad after the match, not during play. This externalizes the error and prevents it from contaminating the next serve.

## Integrate the habits in practice  

1. **Warm‑up** – Run through the between‑point routine while hitting easy balls.  
2. **Serve drills** – Practice the serve ritual under simulated pressure (e.g., score 0‑30, 15‑40).  
3. **Target games** – Play points where the only goal is to land the ball in a pre‑chosen target zone.  

Doing this on court makes the habits automatic when the stakes rise. For a deeper program, the [mental performance for tennis](/mental-performance/tennis) page outlines a full training plan, and the [course](/course) adds video demos.

Giannis Notaras’ book *Overcoming Mental Blocks* breaks down mental habits into bite‑size actions, exactly the kind you need to stop choking in tennis. Use its worksheets to track your routine compliance after each match.

**Takeaway:**  
Reset breath, anchor phrase, target visual, repeat between points. Keep the serve ritual identical every time. Focus on where the ball lands, not how you swing. Treat each point as a fresh start. Master these habits and the choke disappears.
