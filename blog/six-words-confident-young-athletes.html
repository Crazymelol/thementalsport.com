Six Words That Build Confident Young Athletes

PUBLISHED: 2026-07-27

The six words that build confident young athletes are simple: "I love to watch you play." When researchers surveyed college athletes about what their parents said that meant the most, that line came out on top. Not "great game." Not advice. Just an unconditional statement that your kid is worth watching, win or lose.

Bruce Brown and Rob Miller spent years asking athletes to look back on their youth sports careers. The phrase that landed best was not a compliment about talent or a breakdown of a big play. It was six plain words that made kids feel their parent was in their corner no matter the result.

Why These Six Words Do So Much

Most of what parents say around sports, even the kind stuff, quietly grades the child. "Great goal" praises the goal. "You played awesome" praises the performance. Both feel good, and both teach a subtle lesson: my parent's warmth tracks how I did.

"I love to watch you play" breaks that link. There is nothing to earn in it. It says the watching itself is the joy, which frees a kid from the job of performing for your approval. A child who is not performing for approval plays looser, takes more risks, and recovers from mistakes faster.

It also survives a loss. You can say it after a blowout defeat and mean every word, because it was never about the score.

Say It, Then Stop

The power is in the full stop. "I love to watch you play, but you have to hustle back on defense" deletes the whole gift. The moment a "but" arrives, the kid hears the critique and forgets the love.

So deliver the six words clean. No coaching tail. No teachable moment stapled on. Let it be the entire message and walk away while it is still landing.

A Short List of Phrases That Build Kids Up

When you want more than the one line, aim at effort, attitude, and things they controlled:

  • "You never stopped competing out there."
  • "I could tell you were really focused today."
  • "You were a good teammate to [name]."
  • "That took guts."
  • "How did that feel?"

These work because they praise what a kid can repeat on purpose, which is the core of durable confidence. It is the same principle in how to build confidence in young athletes: reward the process, and the process keeps coming back.

The Phrases That Quietly Tear Them Down

  • "Why can't you play like [teammate]?" Comparison teaches a kid their worth is a ranking.
  • "You're the best one out there." Sets a bar they now have to defend every week.
  • "We didn't drive all this way for that." Ties your investment to their output.
  • "You should have scored on that." Turns you into a second referee.

None of these are said with cruelty. They slip out. Knowing them by name makes them easier to catch before they leave your mouth.

The Six Words Are a Habit, Not a Trick

One warm sentence does not build a confident kid on its own. It works because it is repeated, especially in the moments the scoreboard says they did not deserve warmth. Said only after wins, it becomes another reward for winning. Said after the ugly losses too, it becomes proof that your love is not for sale.

The Takeaway

Six words, said often and said clean, do more for a young athlete's confidence than a season of tactical advice. Tell your kid you love to watch them play. Then let it be enough.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the six words to say to your young athlete?+
'I love to watch you play.' When researchers asked college athletes what their parents said that felt best, this line topped the list. It ties your love to them, not to the scoreboard.
Why does 'I love to watch you play' work so well?+
It carries no performance judgment. It tells a kid your approval isn't riding on the result, which lowers pressure and makes it safe to take risks and make mistakes.
What should I not say to a young athlete?+
Avoid comparisons, 'you should have' critiques, and praise aimed only at winning or natural talent. Those quietly teach a kid that your attention depends on results.
How often should I say it?+
Often, and especially after tough games. The line matters most exactly when the scoreboard would suggest they didn't earn it.
