What to Eat Before Competition (and Why Your Nerves Change It)

PUBLISHED: 2026-09-07

What to eat before competition is simple: choose a light, low‑fiber, moderate‑protein snack 60‑90 minutes ahead, pair it with steady hydration, and rehearse the exact same meal in training. Nerves speed up gastric emptying and crank up acid, so the same food that feels fine in practice can turn toxic on game day if you don’t adjust timing and composition.

Why nerves wreck your digestion

When anxiety spikes, the sympathetic nervous system fires. It diverts blood from the gut, tightens the sphincter, and releases stress hormones that increase stomach acidity. The result is bloating, cramping, or the dreaded “butterflies” that turn into a full‑blown upset. Knowing this physiology lets you outsmart it.

Timing is everything

  • 90 minutes pre‑event – Eat a small carbohydrate‑protein combo. This window lets glucose enter the bloodstream while the stomach has time to clear.
  • 30 minutes pre‑event – Sip a half‑liter of water or an electrolyte drink. Avoid large volumes that sit heavy.
  • 15 minutes or less – Stick to a quick mouth rinse of water or a few sips of a sports drink. Anything solid can trigger nausea.

If you push the meal too close to the start, the nervous system will still be in high gear and digestion stalls. If you eat too early, you risk a dip in energy as blood sugar falls.

Foods that settle a nervous stomach

  1. Banana + a spoonful of nut butter – Easy carbs, potassium, and a touch of fat that slows digestion without weighing you down.
  2. Plain Greek yogurt + honey – Protein and simple sugar, plus probiotics that calm gut motility.
  3. Rice cakes topped with avocado – Low‑fiber carbs, healthy fat, and a smooth texture that rarely irritates.
  4. Oatmeal made with water, a dash of cinnamon – Warm, bland, and steady‑release carbs.
  5. Smoothie with whey protein, a handful of berries, and almond milk – Liquid form bypasses the need for heavy chewing.

Avoid high‑fat, high‑fiber, or spicy foods. They linger, increase gas, and amplify the gut’s response to stress.

Hydration strategy that won’t backfire

Dehydration spikes heart rate and magnifies anxiety. Yet over‑drinking floods the stomach. Aim for 5‑7 ml of water per kilogram of body weight spread over the day, then taper to 150‑200 ml every 20 minutes in the hour before competition. Include electrolytes if you sweat heavily; sodium helps retain fluid and prevents cramping.

Practising the competition meal

Your body learns by repetition. If you always fuel with a banana‑nut butter combo in training, the gut will recognize it as safe on the day. Skip the rehearsal and you’ll surprise yourself with a stomach revolt. Treat the pre‑event meal like a warm‑up drill: same timing, same portion, same setting if possible.

The pre‑event fuelling plan (step‑by‑step)

  1. Plan your menu 48 hours ahead – Write down the exact snack, portion size, and drink.
  2. Test it in a mock session – Replicate the competition schedule, including warm‑up and mental prep. Note any gut discomfort.
  3. Adjust based on feedback – If you felt sluggish, cut 10 % of the carbs; if you felt hungry, add a teaspoon of nut butter.
  4. Finalize the timing – Set a timer for 90 minutes before the start. This is your cue to eat.
  5. Prepare a hydration schedule – Mark water breaks at –60, –30, and –15 minutes. Use a bottle with volume markings to stay precise.
  6. Day‑of checklist – Pack the snack in a resealable bag, keep the drink in a insulated bottle, and review the timeline during your pre‑game routine.

Follow this protocol every competition for at least three events and your gut will adapt. The consistency eliminates the surprise factor that nerves love to exploit.

Managing nerves directly

Even the best fuel can be undone by a panic surge. Use the techniques from our guide on how to stop choking under pressure to calm the sympathetic response. Simple breathing patterns, such as a 4‑7‑8 inhale‑hold‑exhale, reduce cortisol and give the stomach a chance to settle.

Building a pre‑game routine that includes nutrition

A solid routine anchors the body and mind. Check out how to build a pre‑game routine for a step‑by‑step framework that slots in your fuelling plan, mental cues, and equipment check. When the routine is automatic, you free up mental bandwidth for performance.

When things go wrong

  • Sudden nausea – Pause, sip water, and focus on a grounding technique. If symptoms persist, switch to a bland electrolyte gel instead of solid food.
  • Unexpected cramp – Lightly stretch the abdomen, apply gentle pressure, and sip a small amount of a sodium‑rich drink.
  • Energy dip – A quick 15‑gram carbohydrate gel can restore glucose without overloading the stomach.

The science behind the plan

Research on exercise nutrition shows that carbohydrate intake 30‑90 minutes before activity improves performance, while high‑fiber meals impair it. Studies on stress physiology confirm that the sympathetic surge delays gastric emptying. Combining these insights gives a practical recipe for athletes who feel their stomach turn before the start.

Takeaway

Eat a light, low‑fiber snack 60‑90 minutes before you compete, hydrate steadily, and rehearse the exact same meal in training. Pair the food plan with a simple breathing cue and a repeatable pre‑game routine. Your gut will stop sabotaging you, and your performance will finally match your effort.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far in advance should I eat my pre‑competition snack?+
Aim for 60‑90 minutes before the start. This gives enough time for digestion while keeping glucose available.
Can I drink coffee before a competition?+
A small cup is fine if you’re used to it, but avoid large amounts. Caffeine can increase stomach acid and worsen anxiety.
What if I feel hungry right before the event?+
Stick to a quick carbohydrate source like a sports gel or a few bites of a banana. Avoid heavy proteins or fats.
How much water should I drink on competition day?+
Target 5‑7 ml per kilogram of body weight throughout the day, then sip 150‑200 ml every 20 minutes in the hour before you compete.
Should I change my meal if I’m competing in the heat?+
Yes. Add electrolytes and a slightly larger carbohydrate portion to compensate for sweat losses.
