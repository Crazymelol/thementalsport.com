First Competition of the Season: How to Handle the Rust

PUBLISHED: 2026-08-27

The first competition of the season is a data day, not a make‑or‑break test. Treat it like a lab experiment: record what feels off, align expectations with your current training age, and run the rust‑day reset so panic never gets a foothold.

Why the first competition of the season feels worse than it is

Your body remembers the last race, not the last gym session. After weeks off, motor patterns are slightly out of sync, heart rate spikes faster, and the crowd’s noise feels louder. That’s normal. The brain amplifies those cues because it expects a performance level you haven’t hit in months.

You’re not slower because you’ve lost talent. You’re slower because you’re still calibrating timing, breathing, and confidence. Recognize the mismatch, and you remove the story that you’re “ruined.”

Resetting expectations to match your training age

  1. Audit your recent training – List the volume, intensity, and frequency of the last four weeks.
  2. Translate that into a performance band – If you’ve been at 70 % of last season’s load, expect to hit roughly 70 % of your previous race times.
  3. Write it down – A concrete number stops the mind from inventing worst‑case scenarios.

When you set a realistic band, every deviation becomes data, not failure.

The rust‑day reset: a step‑by‑step protocol

Step 1 – Ground the body

  • Perform three controlled diaphragmatic breaths.
  • Follow with a physiological sigh: inhale deeply through the nose, add a second quick inhale, then exhale slowly through the mouth. This trick, popularized by Andrew Huberman, drops heart rate in seconds.

Step 2 – Self‑distanced self‑talk

  • Say to yourself, “I am observing a race, not defining my worth.”
  • Use the technique championed by Ethan Kross to create mental distance and prevent catastrophizing.

Step 3 – Micro‑goal framing

  • Break the event into 10‑second chunks.
  • Focus only on the next chunk, not the entire distance.

Step 4 – Data capture

  • Jot down split times, perceived effort, and any technical hiccups on a small notepad or phone app.
  • Treat the notes as lab results, not grades.

Step 5 – Post‑event debrief

  • Within 30 minutes, review the data.
  • Highlight three things that worked, two that need tweaking, and one actionable adjustment for the next race.

Follow these five moves and you turn rust into a diagnostic session rather than a disaster.

Treat the event as a data collection day

Think of the first competition of the season as a field test. You wouldn’t launch a new product without measuring temperature, pressure, and user feedback. Same principle applies to sport.

  • Timing – Record every split, even if you feel it’s “off.”
  • Physiology – Note heart‑rate zones, breathing patterns, and any spikes.
  • Psychology – Mark moments when anxiety spikes or confidence surges.

When you have hard numbers, you can compare them to training logs and see exactly where the rust sits.

Routines that keep rust from turning into panic

A pre‑game routine is a mental anchor. It tells your nervous system, “We’ve done this before; it’s safe.” Build one that fits your sport, then rehearse it daily. For ideas, check out the guide on how to build a pre‑game routine.

During the warm‑up, incorporate a quick version of the rust‑day reset. The combination of breath work and self‑distanced talk steadies the nervous system before the starter pistol fires.

If you feel the urge to choke, switch to the technique in our article on how to stop choking under pressure. A single cue, like “anchor” or “steady”, can pull you out of the spiral.

Quick tools for nervous moments

  • Box breathing – 4 seconds in, 4 seconds hold, 4 seconds out, 4 seconds hold. Use it at the start line.
  • Anchor phrase – Choose a two‑word mantra (“steady focus,” “forward motion”) and repeat it silently on each stride.
  • Visual cue – Spot a fixed object on the track, lock eyes for a second, then shift focus back to your form.

These tools cost nothing, take seconds, and keep the rust from spreading.

The competition mindset shift

Your brain loves stories. The first competition of the season often triggers the “I’m rusty” narrative. Replace that story with a data‑driven one. You are a scientist, not a performer. Your job is to observe, record, and adjust.

Reading “The Competition Protocol” by Giannis Notaras gives you a blueprint for turning every race into a learning experiment. It reinforces the same principle: performance improves when you treat each event as feedback, not final judgment.

Takeaway – Treat the first competition of the season like a lab session. Ground yourself, speak with distance, break the race into micro‑goals, capture data, and debrief fast. Your routine becomes the shield that stops rust from becoming panic.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my timing feel off in the first competition of the season?+
After a break, neuromuscular coordination and cardiovascular efficiency drop slightly, so splits appear slower even though fitness hasn’t vanished.
How can I set realistic expectations after a long layoff?+
Compare your recent training volume and intensity to last season’s, then aim for a performance band that reflects that percentage.
What if I still feel panic despite a pre‑game routine?+
Add the physiological sigh and self‑distanced self‑talk from the rust‑day reset; they directly lower heart rate and create mental distance.
Should I treat my first race as a win or a learning opportunity?+
Treat it as a learning opportunity. Recording data and debriefing turns every result into actionable insight for future races.
